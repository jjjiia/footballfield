Experiments in Understanding Scale

I changed the way I think about scale when my son asked how many seconds it was until the weekend. The length of one second was a tangible thing to a child, equivalent to a tick tock sound he can make on his own. Similarly, explanations of large numbers often worked only when they started with the small and personal — the size of his room, the distance walked every morning to school, the height at the top of the slide at his playground.

When I trimmed my son’s nails, I like to hold up a sliver of baby fingernail and say “this is how much the tectonic plates have moved since we last cut your nails”. All because I had read in a decade-old article that “Continents Split Up at the Same Speed Finger Nails Grow.” That factoid stays with me because the sentence connected this global phenomenon so effectively to my literal fingertip. It reminds me that nothing is too big or small to be felt and seen if only we had the right unit of measurement.

“The correlation of some target to some comparator or unit”

Many units of measurement originate in our own bodies - hand, foot, hair, nose, thumb, finger, arm, step. In some cultures they still persist against standards, it is perhaps not only more intuitive but also logical to use our biological attributes as units. After all, part of the world we aim to measure - our built environment, grows out of the human body as its base unit.

Standardized units and systems of measurement are about human communication. While they often have to separate from their human origins in order to function properly, a shared unit builds connection between 2 acts of measurement. It does not matter how different the things we measure are. In loosely calling units in the following experiments colloquial, my intention is not to erase the systems and standards. Instead, I would like to think more about the colloquial unit as a narrative manipulation of standardized units, where straightforward conversions and the world of arms, hairs, and nails coexist.
